Machine Learning for Biology | Day 61

Six chapters. Six models. Here is where we have been.

Chapter 1 was logistic regression, the simplest useful model, a straight line drawn through a decision.

Chapter 2 was a single decision tree, a model you can read out loud, one split at a time.

Chapter 3 was a random forest, many trees voting together, each one blind to what the others saw.

Chapter 4 was gradient boosting, trees built one after another, each one correcting what the last one got wrong.

Chapter 5 was a support vector machine, finding the widest possible boundary between two outcomes, not just any line that happened to separate them.

Chapter 6 was a neural network, learning which combinations of positions mattered on its own, rather than being told in advance which ones to multiply together.

Six models. Six different ways of using an answer key to find a pattern. Every one of them, without exception, was handed the correct label during training and asked to reproduce it.

Chapter 7 is not. No label. No answer key. The model sees only the data itself and has to find structure inside it unaided, the way a biologist already does at a bench, sorting cells by what they look like rather than by a name someone assigned in advance. Day 2 of this series raised exactly this contrast and then set it aside for six chapters. This is where it gets picked up.

This is also the last chapter. Six models in, one to go, and Machine Learning for Biology ends here on purpose, at a real stopping point, not because the models ran out.

Chapter 7 starts tomorrow.
